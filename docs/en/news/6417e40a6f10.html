DHS and EOIR finalize rule on civil penalties for failure to depart and illegal entry

Published Oct 9, 2026Effective Oct 14, 2026

DHS and the Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR) have issued a final rule finalizing their June 2025 interim final rule on civil monetary penalties for certain immigration violations. The final rule keeps the prior procedures largely unchanged but adds one new provision allowing late-filed appeals in cases of documented mail delivery delays beyond the alien's control.

Key points

  • The final rule finalizes the interim final rule (IFR) that took effect June 27, 2025.
  • It applies to aliens who willfully fail to depart after a final removal order, aliens who miss a voluntary departure deadline, and aliens apprehended while entering or attempting to enter the U.S. unlawfully.
  • The one change from the IFR allows ICE to accept a late-filed appeal if the delay was due to documented mail delivery problems beyond the alien's control.
  • The rule is effective on the date of its publication in the Federal Register.
  • The agencies state the rule implements directives in Executive Order 14159 and Proclamation 10935 (Project Homecoming) aimed at enforcing removal and voluntary departure obligations.

Who is affected

Aliens subject to final removal orders, voluntary departure orders, or apprehended for unlawful entry are affected.

What to do

Affected individuals should review any penalty notices carefully and consult an immigration attorney regarding appeal rights and deadlines.

One such authority is civil monetary penalties. As explained in more detail in the IFR, Congress has authorized DHS to impose civil penalties on: (1) aliens who willfully fail or refuse to depart the United States after a final order of removal or take other action to thwart removal; (2) aliens who fail to comply with a voluntary departure order; and (3) aliens who are apprehended entering or attempting to enter the United States unlawfully (“unlawful entry penalties”). See INA 274D(a)(1), 8 U.S.C. 1324d(a)(1); INA 240B(d)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C. 1229c(d)(1)(A); INA 275(b)(1), 8 U.S.C. 1325(b)(1). Congress has also specified the authorized range of penalty amounts, which DHS has adjusted for inflation. For “unlawful entry,” the penalty amounts range from $100 to $500 per entry or attempted entry. See INA 275(b)(1), 8 U.S.C. 1325(b)(1); 8 CFR 280.53(b)(14). For failure to comply with voluntary departure conditions, the penalty is set by an Immigration Judge or the Board of Immigration Appeals (“BIA”) and may range from $1,992 to $9,970 after inflation adjustments, but the general default amount is $3,000. See INA 240B(d)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C. 1229c(d)(1)(A); 8 CFR 280.53(b)(3), 1240.26(l). For aliens ordered removed who remain in the United States, DHS may impose a penalty of up to $998 for each day the alien willfully fails to comply with the order. See INA 274D(a)(1), 8 U.S.C. 1324d(a)(1); 8 CFR 280.53(b)(14). As the IFR explains, DHS believes that robust application of these civil penalties will deter future unlawful entries and encourage greater compliance with removal and voluntary departure orders, in line with Congress’s intent and this Administration’s focus on securing the border and restoring integrity to the immigration system. See 90 FR 27439–40, 27442.
Prior to the IFR, however, DHS was required to use the general civil penalty procedures at 8 CFR part 280 to impose these penalties. These procedures were promulgated by the former Immigration and Naturalization Service (“INS”) decades before Congress authorized the specific failure-to-depart and unlawful entry penalties covered by the IFR. See 90 FR 27440–41. Those regulations were designed primarily for penalties imposed on airlines and other carriers that violate INA provisions governing the transport of aliens into the United States, rather than for penalties against individual aliens for failure-to-depart or unlawful entry. Id. Under those regulations, DHS had to provide a written notice of intent to fine (“NIF”), served in person or by certified mail, and then wait at least 30 days for a response. Id. If the alien did not respond or did not contest the fine, a DHS officer could issue a final decision imposing the penalty. Id. If the alien contested the fine, the alien could request an in-person interview or submit a sworn written response; a supervisory immigration officer would then issue a written decision; the alien could seek reconsideration within DHS; after exhausting DHS procedures, the alien could appeal to the BIA. Id. As discussed in the IFR, these procedures are lengthy, resource intensive, and not tailored to the failure-to-depart and unlawful entry penalties that Congress later authorized. See 90 FR 27444–46.
The IFR streamlined and simplified the process DHS uses to impose civil penalties for failure-to-depart and unlawful entry and eliminated unnecessary procedures that are not required by statute. See 90 FR 27449–52. The IFR changed only the way DHS issues and adjudicates civil monetary penalties and the way an alien may contest such penalties for these specific fines. 90 FR 27454. The IFR did not require the imposition of any new penalties or otherwise change the substantive criteria for issuing civil penalties. Id.

Before issuing a penalty, ICE guidance requires the immigration officer to verify that there are no DHS records of the alien’s departure from the United States and reviews the alien’s immigration records for information suggesting that the alien’s failure to depart was not willful or voluntary. This includes information about: automatic stays resulting from motions to reopen; certain immigration benefits under the Violence Against Women Act (“VAWA”) that stay the alien’s removal; for aliens on an order of supervision, efforts the alien has made to secure travel documents at DHS’s request; whether DHS has affirmatively approved the alien for deferred action; whether the Immigration Courts, DHS, or a federal court has granted a stay of removal; and whether DHS or EOIR has granted the alien protection that excuses the alien’s failure to depart while the alien maintains that protection, including temporary protected status (“TPS”), protection under the Convention Against Torture (“CAT”), and withholding of removal under INA 241(b)(3), 8 U.S.C. 1231(b)(3) when no alternative country of removal is available. Absent evidence in records accessible to DHS showing that the alien is permitted to remain in the United States, or that the alien has departed, DHS believes the statutory prerequisites to issue a penalty will have been met where an alien ordered removed or granted voluntary departure is physically present in the United States beyond the time frame specified by the Immigration Judge. As the IFR explains, civil penalties for failure to depart are generally issued after completion of section 240 removal proceedings that resulted in a final order requiring the alien’s departure from the United States immediately or within a certain time frame specified by the Immigration Judge. See 90 FR 27440. These aliens have received due process through section 240 removal proceedings where they had an opportunity to contest the charges against them, to seek relief, and to be informed by an Immigration Judge of their obligation to depart and of the consequences of failing to do so. Id. Aliens granted voluntary departure prior to the conclusion of section 240 removal proceedings have already agreed to forgo challenging removal or seeking relief in exchange for being allowed to depart voluntarily. Id. In these circumstances, and when DHS records show the removal order is executable, the Departments reasoned that an alien’s continued presence in the United States is generally sufficient to establish liability for these civil penalties, absent the alien providing evidence on appeal14 to demonstrate otherwise. See 90 FR 27442–43.
In addition, Immigration Judges are required by law to inform aliens of their departure obligations and the consequences for failing to comply. Under INA 240(c)(5), 8 U.S.C. 1229(a)(5), when an Immigration Judge orders removal, the Immigration Judge must inform the alien of the consequences for failure to depart under the order of removal, including civil penalties described at sections INA 274D, 8 U.S.C. 1324(d). These statutory and regulatory requirements are directly relevant to the willfulness and voluntariness elements in the civil penalty statutes. Before DHS considers imposing a failure to depart penalty, the alien will ordinarily have been advised, on the record, that the law requires the alien to depart the United States and that failure to do so may result in civil fines and other adverse consequences. When an alien remains in the United States after such clear, legally required notice, his or her continued presence strongly supports the conclusion that the alien has “willfully fail[ed] or refuse[d]” to depart under a removal order or “voluntarily” failed to comply with a voluntary departure order, as required by the failure to depart civil penalty statutes. In making this assessment, DHS also relies on the presumption of regularity that attaches to official acts of government officers, including Immigration Judges. See United States Postal Serv. v. Gregory, 534 U.S. 1, 10 (2001). Under this presumption, DHS may reasonably assume that Immigration Judges complied with their legal obligations under the INA and implementing regulations and provided the required advisals concerning departure obligations and the consequences of noncompliance, absent specific evidence to the contrary in the record. In all cases, ICE considers the willfulness of an alien’s violation in determining whether to impose a penalty.
Taken together—(1) the procedural protections provided in section 240 removal proceedings, (2) the mandatory Immigration Judge advisals of departure obligations and penalties, (3) the presumption of regularity applicable to those advisals, and (4) DHS’s careful review of U.S. Government records to confirm that the alien is subject to a final, executable order and lacks lawful permission to remain—DHS concludes that an alien’s continued presence in the United States is generally sufficient to establish the willful or voluntary failure to depart required to impose these civil penalties, absent evidence provided by the alien on appeal to demonstrate that the alien is not liable. Therefore, the Departments reasoned that streamlining the issuance and adjudication of these civil penalties, and the associated appeals process, aligns more closely with the nature and intent of these straightforward civil penalties. While in most cases, DHS records sufficiently establish the facts necessary to demonstrate that the alien is subject to these civil penalties, the alien retains the right to contest them on limited grounds. See 90 FR 27442–46.
To the extent an alien believes that DHS issued a penalty erroneously—for example, because the alien is not subject to a removal order, is legally permitted to remain in the United States, or did not willfully or voluntarily fail to depart—the Departments determined that the IFR provides sufficient notice and an opportunity to contest the decision before DHS, and that further review before EOIR is unnecessary. See 90 FR 27451–55. Under the IFR, a DHS immigration officer must issue a written decision that sets forth the statutory and factual basis for the penalty and informs the alien of the requirements and deadlines for filing an appeal. See Mullane v. Cent. Hanover Bank & Tr. Co., 339 U.S. 306, 314 (1950) (stating that notice under due process must be of a sufficient nature as to reasonably convey the required information). The revised appeal process also preserves an alien’s opportunity to contest a civil penalty. Under the IFR’s procedures, the alien has 15 business days to appeal using the DHS-developed Form 281, Notice and Violation of Order, and a supervisory immigration officer who did not issue the initial decision will review the record de novo and may request additional evidence before making a final determination. See 90 FR 27452–54.
As explained in the IFR and in response to comments in Section III of this preamble, the Departments believe that additional procedures, beyond those contained in the IFR, would not enhance the accuracy, fairness, or reliability of the process given the straightforward nature of these penalties and the limited grounds that an alien may have to contest them. See id. Thus, for the reasons discussed in the IFR and in response to comments in Section III of this preamble, the Departments believe that the revised procedure preserves an alien’s ability to contest the fine while better ensuring that the BIA does not unnecessarily expend resources in areas where DHS is better positioned to review such determinations, and that DHS can reach final decisions efficiently and apply these statutory penalties swiftly and at the scale necessary to address the large number of aliens who have entered or remain unlawfully in the United States. One commenter considered such civil penalties necessary to deter unlawful presence, encourage self-departure, and help offset the costs associated with enforcing immigration laws and carrying out removals. Another commenter recognized the government’s interest in recovering some of the costs associated with removing aliens. Collectively, these comments broadly endorsed the IFR’s underlying policy objective of streamlining the process for imposing civil monetary penalties on aliens subject to the violations addressed in this rule. Response: The Departments agree that promoting compliance with the Nation’s immigration laws and maintaining border security is critical, and this rule is a key part of DHS’s efforts to use all statutorily available tools to help achieve those objectives consistent with the Administration’s policies and priorities while also allowing EOIR to focus time and resources on completing cases that are more aligned with its statutory authorities. See 90 FR 8445–55. Specifically, the unprecedented number of aliens entering and remaining in the country illegally, in recent years necessitates this rule. See 90 FR 27442 (quoting 90 FR 8443). Congress has authorized DHS to impose civil monetary penalties on aliens who unlawfully enter the United States and those who remain after a final removal or voluntary departure order. See 90 FR 27439–41. As explained in the IFR, this rule allows DHS to preserve due process while more efficiently imposing a greater number of these civil penalties on the aliens subject to them. 90 FR 27442. The Departments believe that this effort will, in turn, encourage greater compliance with removal and voluntary departure orders and discourage future unlawful entries. Id.
B. General Opposition
1. Impacts on aliens, families, businesses, and communities Comment: Many commenters opposed the IFR, asserting that it will negatively impact aliens and their families. One commenter stated that the aliens impacted by this rule could face steep civil monetary fines in excess of $1,000,000, causing significant financial distress for them and their families. Other commenters echoed this point, stating that the rule creates a substantial financial burden on aliens, and specifically viewed as particularly egregious the fine of $998-per-day for willful failure or refusal to depart after a final removal order and certain other proscribed activities. One commenter said the IFR directly harms U.S. citizens and lawful permanent residents because many aliens subject to these civil monetary fines are parents and spouses of U.S. citizens and permanent residents. They characterized these fines as “economically catastrophic for the entire household” resulting in “eviction, bankruptcy, or forced separation.” This commenter also noted that aliens who fail to pay the assessed penalties may be subject to additional consequences including garnished wages, seizure of assets including homes and vehicles, and long-term financial ruin.
Another commenter reiterated this point and additionally noted the potential confiscation of savings and vehicles and imposed additional financial planning burden and financial uncertainty. This commenter claimed that the Departments’ conclusion that the IFR aligns with due process was arbitrary and capricious. The commenter said that the IFR arbitrarily characterized these penalties as “modest,” and claimed the Departments overlooked case law recognizing heightened due process interests when the government seizes homes, confiscates savings, and imposes fines that result in significant economic impacts on families. Given the substantial penalty amounts and the collateral consequences that can result if an alien fails to pay, the commenter argued that the IFR’s procedures are constitutionally inadequate.
Response: As an initial point, Congress, not the Departments, has defined both the types of violations and the monetary range for the civil penalties covered by the IFR and this rule. These penalties and their amounts reflect Congress’s judgment that the conduct involved—an alien’s unlawful entry and failure to depart after a removal or voluntary departure order—is serious, and in some cases substantial civil fines are necessary to encourage aliens to comply with the immigration laws. See 90 FR 27439–40. This rule does not expand the scope of these penalties, nor their minimum or maximum dollar amounts. Furthermore, the immigration consequences for failing to depart or unlawful entry flow from the underlying immigration violation, not from the failure to pay the assessed civil monetary penalties. This rule merely streamlines the process that DHS applies when imposing civil penalties that Congress has already authorized DHS to impose. Therefore, to the extent that commenters are objecting to these civil monetary penalties and the amounts authorized by Congress, such concerns are outside of the scope of this rulemaking.
DHS often assesses the maximum penalty amount to fulfill the enforcement and deterrent objectives of the statute, as intended by Congress. These amounts are also consistent with the enforcement priorities of the Administration, as reflected in recent Executive Orders. See 90 FR 8443, 8443–45; 90 FR 8467, 8467. However, the Departments note that, in the voluntary departure context, immigration officers follow the civil penalty amounts that will continue to be set by EOIR. See 8 CFR 1240.26(l). The Departments disagree with commenters’ arguments that DHS should not streamline the process for imposing these civil penalties because of the burdens the penalties create for aliens and their families. The Departments acknowledge these concerns but note that financial, emotional, and family burdens are also an inherent consequence of removal based on an alien’s violation of the Nation’s immigration laws. See e.g., Moctezuma-Reyes v. Garland, 124 F.4th 416, 423 (6th Cir. 2024) (“[F]inancial and emotional strains are the typical results of removal; they aren’t rare”); see also Matter of Pilch, 21 I&N Dec. 627, 631 (BIA 1996) (“[E]motional hardship caused by severing family and community ties is a common result of deportation”). The most substantial civil fines apply only to aliens who have been ordered removed or granted voluntary departure after the conclusion of immigration proceedings. These aliens have been warned of the consequences for failing to comply with the removal or departure order but have willfully ignored their departure obligations. In these circumstances, the Departments do not believe that it is unfair to impose substantial civil penalties that Congress has authorized for these violations even though it may cause additional burdens and hardships on aliens and their families.

An alien’s engagement in these activities does not absolve him or her from complying with the immigration laws. See 90 FR 27445–46; Section II of this preamble; see also 90 FR 8444–45.
Regarding comments related to the IFR’s potential effects on the willingness of aliens to report crimes, while DHS does not dispute that illegal aliens might be less willing to report a crime, DHS cannot reliably estimate this rule’s speculative impact on local policing, especially considering that this rule merely provides procedures for issuing civil fines that are authorized by statute. For similar reasons, the Departments decline to rescind the IFR based on other speculative community-level effects described by some commenters. Commenters provided no support for their belief that the revised civil penalty procedures will discourage eligible individuals from accessing education, healthcare, or other community services, or otherwise disengaging from their communities. The Departments believe that any such indirect impacts are also outweighed by this rule’s potential benefits, including benefits to border security, public safety, immigration enforcement, and the integrity of the immigration laws.

C. Justification and Need for the Rule
1. Nature of the civil fines Comment: Several commenters took issue with the IFR’s claim that the straightforward and readily verifiable nature of the failure to depart penalties supports the Departments’ decision to streamline the underlying penalty process. 90 FR 27441–46. Commenters claimed that DHS has erroneously issued many civil fines over the past year under the prior penalty process, including to aliens with temporary or permanent immigration status and with an approved visa petition and related waiver request. A commenter also claimed that DHS has issued fines to aliens with pending motions seeking to reopen or rescind removal orders. One commenter claimed that their client received a NIF that incorrectly stated a voluntary departure order had been issued several years prior to the date of actual issuance. Another alleged that, based on their clients’ experiences, DHS frequently does not use or seek out the relevant information to determine whether aliens are willfully evading a removal order, thus frequently making incorrect assessments of such civil monetary penalties. One commenter stated that DHS appears to be issuing these fines in bulk through an automated system, often based on incomplete or inaccurate records and without considering aliens’ individual circumstances. Collectively, these commenters argued that the number of alleged errors seen in these fines contradict the IFR’s assertion that these penalties are straightforward and readily verifiable and raise concerns that the number of errors will be even higher under the IFR’s streamlined process.
Response: Civil penalties are issued based on the facts of an alien’s case; an alien who chooses to appeal to DHS may present evidence relevant to their specific circumstances. Prior to issuing a failure to depart civil penalty, immigration officers verify there are no applications or actions pending litigation—including appeals, motions, and petitions for review (“PFRs”)—that would affect the finality of a removal order or allow the alien to lawfully remain in the United States. In the jurisdictions in which the filing of a PFR results in an automatic stay of removal, ICE generally will not issue the civil fine until there are no legal impediments to removal. Additionally, when calculating a fine under INA 274D, 8 U.S.C. 1324d, immigration officers account for any periods after the removal order became administratively final during which the alien was authorized to remain in the United States due to, for example, a stay of removal issued by EOIR, DHS, or a federal circuit court of appeals.
DHS rejects the claim that it is issuing these penalties in bulk through “an automated system” without a human verifying that there is a final executable removal order, and there are no legal impediments to removal. DHS further notes that aliens assessed a failure to depart civil penalty may raise on appeal any issues that purportedly affect the finality of the removal order, dates of issuance, or the alien’s willfulness in failing to depart.
Finally, ICE acknowledges that, in some limited number of cases, it mistakenly issued penalties under the pre‑IFR procedures in 8 CFR part 280. However, ICE does not agree that the risk of such errors will increase under the more streamlined IFR procedures that this rule finalizes. The IFR was designed to simplify and speed up the process of issuing and finalizing ICE civil penalty decisions. It does not change ICE’s process for deciding in the first place whether an alien is subject to a civil penalty. Moreover, as discussed above in Section II of this preamble, aliens have appealed civil penalties under both the pre‑IFR and post‑IFR procedures, albeit to different agencies, and have succeeded in having penalties set aside when issued in error at similar rates under both procedures. For these reasons, DHS continues to believe that the IFR provides aliens with sufficient opportunity to challenge a penalty they believe was issued in error and to have those penalties rescinded. The commenters’ examples of errors in a limited number of individual cases under the pre-IFR process do not, on their face, call into question the adequacy of the IFR’s process for error-correction. See Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. 319, 344 (1976) (finding that “procedural due process rules are shaped by the risk of error inherent in the truth-finding process as applied to the generality of cases.”).

Removal of notice of intent to fine Comment: Several commenters asserted that eliminating the notice of intent to fine (NIF) erodes procedural protections by transforming what was once a preliminary document signaling DHS’s intent to issue a fine into a final agency decision. Commenters stated that this change reduces the time that aliens have to understand and respond to fines, seek legal advice, and submit an appeal. Additionally, one commenter suggested that eliminating the NIF process—which the commenter characterizes as being designed to notify aliens of potential penalties and allowing them to rectify the violation—is inconsistent with the goal of incentivizing aliens to comply with removal and departure orders.
Response: The Departments disagree with commenters’ characterization of the rule. Although the IFR eliminated the NIF process for these penalties, it continues to require written notice of penalties that identify the factual and legal basis for liability, see 8 CFR 281.1(c), and provides aliens with an opportunity to submit a written response with supporting evidence before a final determination is made, see 8 CFR 281.1(e). Indeed, the differences between the NIF process prior to the IFR under 8 CFR part 280 and the process established by the IFR under 8 CFR part 281 are less substantial than commenters suggest. Under both regulations, if an alien fails to respond to the NIF or the penalty decision, as applicable, the alien will be liable for the penalty. See generally 90 FR 27449–54 (comparing 8 CFR 280 and 8 CFR 281). The only difference is that, under the part 280 process, a supervisory officer must convert a NIF into a final unappealable decision; under the IFR, the penalty decision automatically becomes final if the alien does not appeal. Compare 8 CFR 280.13(a)(2), with 8 CFR 281.1(f)(3). Under both regulations, an alien who timely appeals a penalty decision is entitled to additional process before a final decision is issued. Compare 8 CFR 280.13(b), with 8 CFR 281.1(e). Therefore, the IFR merely converts the prior two-stage NIF process under 8 CFR part 280 into a single process that eliminates duplicate administrative steps and paperwork if an alien does not appeal, but the fundamental safeguards of notice and an opportunity to be heard remain unchanged. Id.
The Departments acknowledge that the IFR reduces the amount of time that an alien has to contest a penalty and to submit supporting evidence compared to the NIF process. Prior to the IFR an alien generally was required to answer a NIF within 30 calendar days and could submit supporting evidence at an in-person hearing, if requested. 8 CFR 280.12. Under the IFR, an alien must file an appeal postmarked within 15 business days of the date of service of the civil penalty decision and the alien is generally required to submit all evidence in support of the appeal at the time of filing. 8 CFR 281.1(e)(1). However, as explained in the IFR and in response to related comments in this preamble, the Departments believe that shortening the timeline for appeals and evidentiary submissions is appropriate for the failure to depart and unlawful entry penalties due to the straightforward and readily determinable nature of these penalties.
Moreover, the Departments note that in some respects the IFR simplified the process for aliens to contest civil penalties compared to the procedures under 8 CFR part 280. An alien may trigger an appeal under the 8 CFR 281.1 process by simply filing a notice of written appeal indicating that the alien is contesting the penalty. Indeed, DHS uses the Form 281, Notice of Violation and Order, which includes a field for aliens to use to appeal a penalty. The alien may, but is not required to, submit written argument or documentary evidence contesting the penalty. Id. In contrast, under the NIF process, the alien must provide a written defense, in duplicate, under oath setting forth the reasons why a fine should not be imposed. See 8 CFR 280.12.

3. Service by first class mail Comment: Several commenters claimed that the change in service of process raises due process concerns. One commenter observed that the rule permits DHS to unilaterally send fines by ordinary mail despite previously issuing NIFs by personal service or certified mail. Another commenter states that previous rules required that communications be sent by certified mail or served in person, guaranteeing receipt, and that the IFR removing this provision to instead allow service by ordinary mail will dramatically increase the likelihood that intended recipients will not receive information about their fine, miss their appeal window, and unknowingly accrue large amounts in civil monetary penalties. Another commenter suggests that these risks are amplified in this context given the highly transient nature of the population subject to these fines. One commenter explained that service by regular mail will not reasonably apprise aliens of the fine because regular mail makes it significantly less likely that the alien will receive the decision in a timely manner if at all. This commenter also points out that in the removal proceedings context in which DHS uses service by regular mail a significant amount of the information provided in mailings is also available online unlike in this civil penalty context. They state that here, in this context, aliens have no other way to access the information or be on notice that they have penalties brought against them. They also note that this is particularly an issue for those who have been fined incorrectly and explain that since the beginning of 2025 they have heard from permanent residents who have been in the United States for decades and would have no reason to believe there are pending proceedings against them who received NIFs. Other commenters also raised that authorizing service by regular mail increases the likelihood of delay or non-receipt, one noting that regular mail cannot be tracked.
Another commenter claimed that the IFR fails to explain how ordinary mail saves money and staff time compared to personal service or certified mail. The commenter also noted that the IFR does not explain why ICE cannot use personal service for aliens with removal orders because, as the IFR notes, these aliens are required to attend in-person check-ins with ICE.
Response: The Departments disagree that permitting service of civil monetary penalty determinations by regular mail raises due process concerns or fails to provide constitutionally adequate notice. Due process does not require use of any particular method of service or guarantee of actual receipt. Rather, it requires that the chosen method be “reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances,” to inform interested parties of the action and to permit them an opportunity to respond. Mullane, 339 U.S. at 314 (stating that notice under due process must be of a sufficient nature as to reasonably convey the required information). Service by regular mail has long been recognized as a constitutionally sufficient method of notice in civil, administrative, and immigration contexts, including in removal proceedings, and other DHS communications. See e.g., Gardner v. City of Columbus, Ohio, 841 F.2d 1272, 1279 (6th Cir. 1988) (recognizing that service by regular mail satisfied due process notice requirements, while among other factors, noting government’s observation that service by certified mail would be more costly); Urbina-Osejo v. INS, 124 F.3d 1314, 1317 (9th Cir.1997) (stating that “notice of a deportation hearing sent by regular mail to the last address provided by the alien to the INS satisfies the requirements of constitutional due process”); Matter of Grijalva, 21 I&N Dec. 27 (BIA 1995) (holding that notice sent by regular mail is presumed sufficient if it is properly addressed and mailed according to established procedures and that the presumption of delivery may be rebutted with evidence of non-receipt). The IFR adopted this established practice.
Penalty determinations are sent to the address the alien has provided to DHS or that otherwise appears on file. As the IFR explains, DHS has a reasonable expectation that aliens will take these requirements seriously because failure to do so can result in a range of consequences including criminal penalties. INA 266, 8 U.S.C. 1306. In general, aliens in the United States are under specific statutory and regulatory obligations to register their presence, see INA 261–264, 8 U.S.C. 1301–1304; 8 CFR Part 264, and to keep the U.S. Government apprised of their current address while in the country, see INA 265, 8 U.S.C. 1305; 8 CFR 265.1. Most aliens subject to the monetary penalties covered by this rule are warned of their address obligations upon initiation of section 240 removal proceedings, and DHS is taking steps to ensure that all aliens are aware of, and comply with, registration and address requirements consistent with this Administration’s policies. Additionally, DHS provides convenient and reliable ways for aliens to update their addresses including through online portals, and immigration officers have access to current address data maintained in DHS and EOIR databases.
Although some commenters expressed concern that personal service or certified mail had previously been used for NIFs, the governing statutory provisions do not require the use of those methods for civil monetary penalty notices, and neither do due process principles impose such a requirement. INA 240B(d)(1)(A), 8 U.S.C. 1229c(d)(1)(A); INA 274D(a)(1), 8 U.S.C. 1324d(a)(1); & INA 275(b)(1), 8 U.S.C. 1325(b)(1). See also Mullane, 339 U.S. at 314 (“Personal service has not in all circumstances been regarded as indispensable to the process due to residents, and it has more often been held unnecessary as to nonresidents. We disturb none of the established rules on these subjects.”); Dusenbery v. United States, 534 U.S. 161, 161–62 (2002) (upholding notice sent by certified mail to a prison, even though inmate claimed he never received it, demonstrating that due process does not require proof of actual receipt, and explaining that Mennonite Board of Missions v. Adams, 462 U.S. 791, 797 n.3 (1983) did “not say that the State must provide actual notice, but that it must attempt to provide actual notice”) (emphasis in original).45 Furthermore, even a Notice to Appear (NTA), which initiates section 240 removal proceedings and thus carries greater legal consequences, may be served by regular mail. INA 239(a)(1), 8 U.S.C. 1229(a)(1). Although the statute previously required certified mail, Congress removed that requirement in IIRIRA, expressly permitting service by other forms of mail. See INA 239(a)(1)–(2), as amended by 45 The agency acknowledges that in Jones v. The INA now provides that service by mail “shall be sufficient if there is proof of attempted delivery to the last address provided by the alien.” INA 239(c), 8 U.S.C. 1229(c). By contrast, the INA is silent as to the method of service for civil monetary penalties under INA 240B(d), 274D(a), 275(b), 8 U.S.C. 1229c(d), 1324d(a), 1325(b). Accordingly, this final rule will allow DHS to use routine service, including regular mail which in turn will lessen DHS’s administrative burdens, such as the time and resources needed for personal service and the additional costs and increased processing times associated with certified mail. Routine service also provides an alien appropriate notice of the imposition of a civil monetary penalty.
The Departments also disagree that service by regular mail increases the likelihood that aliens will unknowingly accrue penalties or be deprived of an opportunity to appeal. First, the rule’s procedures are reasonably designed to prevent aliens from unknowingly accruing civil monetary penalties. Under INA 262, 8 U.S.C. 1302, most aliens present in the United States for 30 days or more are required to apply for registration and fingerprinting. As a result, most aliens are subject to the address update requirement in INA 265, 8 U.S.C. 1305. Moreover, most aliens subject to the penalties addressed in this rule are specifically advised of these obligations upon initiation of section 240 removal proceedings. INA 239(a)(1)(F), 8 U.S.C. 1229(a)(1)(F). DHS provides convenient and reliable ways for aliens to update their addresses, including use of online portals, and immigration officers have access to current address data maintained in DHS and EOIR databases. 90 FR 27452.
Against that backdrop and as noted in the IFR, the Supreme Court has recognized that service by regular mail may, in some circumstances, provide better notice than certified mail or other methods. Jones, 547 U.S. at 234–35. This rule adopts that approach. 90 FR 27453. Based on ICE’s experience, service by ordinary mail is often more likely to reach aliens, including those who may be attempting to evade detection or who have moved without updating their address. Id. Such aliens, or members of their household, may be less likely to answer the door for personal service or to accept certified mail, whereas regular mail is delivered directly to their mailbox. Id. Even when an alien has moved, regular mail may still result in the current occupant forwarding address information or otherwise alerting the intended recipient. Id. (citing Jones, 547 U.S. at 235). Moreover, the rule does not mandate use of regular mail in all cases. DHS retains discretion to use other methods of service where appropriate. 90 FR 27453 (citing 8 CFR 281.1(d)). Accordingly, DHS reasonably believes that notices sent to the most recent address provided by the alien satisfy due process requirements.
Secondly, the rule includes sufficient procedural protections that mitigate the effects of any variation in mail delivery times and afford a meaningful opportunity for appeal. Consistent with 8 CFR 281.1(e), an alien shall file a written notice of appeal with the Department postmarked within 15 business days of the date of service of the decision. Although service of the immigration officer’s decision is complete upon mailing, the rule mitigates the effects of variability in mail delivery times at the appeal stage by defining timeliness based on the postmark date of the appeal rather than the date of receipt. As explained in the IFR, “DHS will calculate the timeliness of an appeal based on the date that the alien’s appeal is postmarked.” 90 FR 27450 (citing 8 CFR 281.1(e)). Under this framework, an alien who mails an appeal within the 15-business-day period is not penalized for postal delays occurring after the appeal is sent. This approach allocates the risk of mail delay at the filing stage to the Government and helps ensure that ordinary variations in mail delivery do not result in forfeiture of appeal rights.
Along with the notice of appeal, an alien may, but is not required to, provide a written defense or documentary evidence, or both, setting forth the reasons why a penalty should not be imposed. Id. After the appeal is filed, a supervisory immigration officer who did not review the initial decision and order must conduct a de novo review and may request supplemental evidence or information, in which case aliens receive an additional 15 days to provide supporting material. 90 FR 27450 (citing 8 CFR 281.1(e)(2)). The officer may, in their discretion, call for additional briefing or written filings from the alien, and the alien will have 15 days from the receipt of that request to provide the information. Id. The officer will also provide the alien with copies of pertinent documents and records relevant to the penalty, if the alien requests, unless they are law enforcement sensitive, or disclosure is prohibited by law. 90 FR 27450 (citing 8 CFR 281.1(e)(3)). Moreover, if the alien files a notice of appeal, the initial civil penalty decision will remain inoperative during the appeal. 90 FR 27450 (citing 8 CFR 281.1(e)(1)).
In addition, the Departments recognize that aliens may, in limited circumstances, experience delayed or untimely delivery of notices served by mail that is outside aliens’ control. To address this concern while preserving the integrity and administrability of the appeals process, this rule provides a narrow, case-by-case mechanism for accepting appeals filed after the 15-day filing period when the alien demonstrates that the delay resulted from mail delivery issues. Under the rule, an alien who files an appeal after the applicable deadline and asserts that the delay was caused by untimely or delayed mail delivery must submit a written explanation and any available documentary evidence supporting that assertion. If, based on the information submitted, ICE determines that the appeal was filed late due to mail delay beyond the alien’s control, ICE will accept the appeal as timely filed and consider it on the merits. By requiring aliens to affirmatively raise mail delay and provide supporting evidence, the rule ensures that aliens are not prejudiced by mail delivery delays beyond their control, while maintaining finality and administrative efficiency.
The Departments acknowledge commenters’ concerns that certain populations may be more mobile or may have limited access to online resources. However, DHS’s service method here is identical to the method used for numerous other immigration- related notices, including those in removal proceedings where similar concerns may exist. Even if mobile, aliens have a statutory duty to register their presence and keep the U.S. Government apprised of their current address, and DHS has a reasonable expectation that aliens will comply with such requirements. INA 266, 8 U.S.C. 1306. While the information in penalty notices is not available online like it is for removal proceedings notices, the constitutionally relevant question is whether the method of service is reasonably calculated to inform, not whether parallel avenues for accessing the same information exist. See Mullane, 339 U.S. at 315 (“The reasonableness and hence the constitutional validity of any chosen method may be defended on the ground that it is in itself reasonably certain to inform those affected.”). The penalty notice itself contains the relevant information: the statutory basis for the penalty, the factual allegations, the amount sought, and clear instructions on how to appeal. See 8 CFR 281.1(c)(1); 90 FR 27449. Due process does not require DHS to maintain a public online portal for civil monetary penalty notices, nor does the lack of such a portal make regular mail constitutionally insufficient. See Mullane, 339 U.S. at 315.
The Departments also disagree with commenters’ suggestion that ICE must use personal service for individuals who attend in-person check-ins. Not all aliens subject to civil penalties are enrolled in programs involving in-person reporting, and requiring personal service in every case would impose significant operational burdens without materially improving notice. Regular mail ensures efficient and consistent delivery of notices for geographically dispersed aliens.
Finally, while one commenter questioned whether ordinary mail saves time or resources, the IFR explains that allowing service by regular mail promotes efficiency. As discussed in the IFR, personal delivery often may require burdensome manual efforts to locate the alien. It may further require the use of multiple DHS agents or officers to appear on location to ensure officer safety, and multiple attempts at personal service if not effectuated on the first attempt, thereby diverting officers from their other duties. 90 FR 27444–45. Service by certified mail involves preparation of individual mailings in each case, including handwriting envelopes, which has proven to be costly and resource intensive and would become even more so as DHS expands its use of these civil penalties. Indeed, ICE estimates that the certified mail requirement costs the agency $23.53 for each civil monetary penalty, which includes certified mail fees, materials, and labor. 90 FR 27444. Consequently, personal service or certified mail imposes higher costs and increases processing times and is unnecessary to satisfy constitutional or statutory notice requirements. See Mullane, 339 U.S. at 306.
The Departments also recognize commenters’ concerns that some individuals, including lawful permanent residents, may have reported receiving penalty-related notices in error. The IFR’s service provisions do not increase the likelihood of incorrect notices. Instead, the procedural framework contains safeguards and corrective mechanisms, including written notice and a mandatory de novo supervisory review over any appeal. The initial civil penalty decision remains inoperative while a timely administrative appeal is pending. This provides structured mechanisms to correct any errors promptly. Any alien who believes a notice was issued in error may file a simple written appeal explaining as much, triggering the supervisory review process. These procedures ensure that aliens have meaningful opportunities to contest errors. Accordingly, the Departments conclude that service by regular mail, coupled with the exception to the 15-business-day deadline to appeal when ICE determines that there was a mail delay under 8 CFR 281.1(e)(2), is a reasonable, appropriate, and constitutionally sufficient method of providing notice of civil monetary penalty determinations under the INA, particularly given the IFR’s procedural safeguards that ensure aliens retain meaningful opportunity to contest penalties. See Mathews v. Eldridge, 424 U.S. at 335.

The revised process continues to provide aliens with written notice of the proposed penalty that identifies the factual and legal basis for the determination and explains the steps to request the review. As with other immigration notices and charging documents, including NTAs, removal orders, voluntary departure orders, and USCIS decision notices, civil monetary determinations are issued in English, and neither the INA nor the Constitution obligates DHS to translate such documents into every alien’s native language. See Ojeda-Calderon v. Holder, 726 F.3d 669, 675 (5th Cir. 2013) (citing Nazarova v. INS, 171 F.3d 478, 483 (7th Cir. 1999) (explaining that due process does not require immigration notices to be in a language the alien can understand). Civil penalty decisions issued under this final rule will continue to include written instructions explaining how to file an appeal. Aliens may also seek assistance from legal representatives, community organizations, or interpreters. Aliens may provide additional documentation if the supervisory officer requests supplemental briefing during de novo review. Accordingly, the Departments conclude that the English language format of penalty notices is consistent with longstanding immigration practice and constitutional requirements as well as DOJ’s memorandum implementing Executive Order 14224 by minimizing non-essential multilingual services, redirecting resources toward English- language education and assimilation, and ensuring compliance with legal obligations through targeted measures where necessary. Aliens also remain free to seek legal representation at any point in the process, including after filing the initial written appeal request. Nothing in the IFR limits a respondent’s ability to obtain counsel or work with legal service providers. The IFR merely standardizes the timeframe for the initial appeal filing while preserving a meaningful opportunity, through mandatory de novo review and a potential supplemental briefing period, to submit evidence or legal argument. Considering these procedural safeguards, the Departments conclude that the 15-day appeal period is a reasonable and appropriate procedural requirement.
In addition, the IFR also only requires that the alien submit a written appeal explaining the basis for contesting the civil penalty, as the grounds on which an alien may contest a civil penalty are limited and generally involve facts uniquely within the alien’s knowledge. As the IFR noted, aliens will ordinarily possess the necessary information to contest these fines. 90 FR 27450. If an alien submits an appeal with assertions that are unsupported by documentary evidence because there was no time for the alien to obtain these documents, then the supervisory immigration officer reviewing the appeal can request the documents during the course of the appeal to verify the validity of the alien’s assertions. Additionally, the fact that it takes an alien longer than 15 days to obtain travel documents is irrelevant because the fines are issued after the time period aliens have been given to obtain travel documents and depart has elapsed. The streamlined procedures are designed to ensure timely resolution of cases and maintain the integrity of enforcement actions. DHS believes the 15-day appeal period, coupled with ICE’s discretion to equitably toll the appeal deadline when ICE determines that there was a mail delay under 8 CFR 281.1(e)(2), strikes an appropriate balance between efficiency and fairness, providing adequate opportunity for aliens to contest violations and demonstrate lawful status or other circumstances that may preclude liability. Removal of in-person hearings Comment: Several commenters claimed that eliminating in person hearings will deprive aliens of due process protections that ensure their appeal is thoroughly presented and fully considered. Citing Mathews v. Eldridge, one commenter said in-person hearings are critical because they allow aliens to present oral evidence and clarify misunderstandings. Another commenter stated that eliminating in-person hearings means that DHS will only review written filings, briefs, and any documentary evidence submitted which, according to the commenter, deprives aliens from a meaningful opportunity to be heard. Another commenter alleged that the limited due process rights under the new process will contribute to an “extremely high” possibility for error. Response: The Departments disagree that eliminating in-person hearings deprives respondents of due process protections or prevents their appeals from being fully and fairly considered. As an initial matter, as discussed above in Section I of this preamble, the Departments note that only four aliens requested an in-person interview of the 450 appeals and over 12,000 civil penalties under the pre-IFR process, indicating that aliens do not place much value on the need for in-person hearings to contest civil penalties. Moreover, under the Mathews balancing test, the question is whether the procedures established by the IFR provide notice reasonably calculated to inform the alien of the action and a meaningful opportunity to be heard before a final decision is issued. The IFR satisfies both requirements. Aliens receive written notice detailing the factual basis and statutory grounds for the penalty and are afforded the opportunity to file an appeal with the option of including a written defense or documentary evidence. The initial appeal requires only a brief written request for review, and aliens may provide additional documentation if the supervisory immigration officer requests supplemental evidence or information. The supervisory officer must conduct a de novo review, ensuring that the respondent’s written submissions and any relevant evidence are fully considered. The Departments also disagree with commenters’ assertions that in-person hearings are necessary to present oral evidence or clarify misunderstandings. Civil monetary penalties under INA 240B(d), 274D, and 275(b), 8 U.S.C. 1229c(d), 1324d, and 1325(b), turn on objective factual bases such as a prior removal order or a failure to comply with terms of voluntary departure that are typically documented in DHS records. Written submissions allow respondents to contest factual inaccuracies, provide additional documentation, or explain circumstances that negate the willfulness of the alien’s failure to depart. As explained in the IFR, an in-person interview would not enhance the fairness or accuracy of the civil monetary penalty process because of the straightforward issues and types of evidence involved in these cases, which do not turn on credibility determinations or require the cross-examination of witnesses. If an alien, through no fault of his or her own, did not receive notice of the removal or voluntary departure order or was prevented from complying due to circumstances beyond his or her control, the alien can provide a written explanation and simple documentary evidence to support the claim. Accordingly, a requirement for in-person hearings would not meaningfully improve the accuracy or fairness of determinations, given the narrow factual issues typically involved in these penalties. See Mathews, 424 U.S. at 343–44 (considering for due process purposes both the nature of the issues to be decided and the nature of the evidence to be presented, such as whether it consists mainly of documents or whether the resolution of the issue hinges on the need for in person testimony).
The risk of error is also mitigated by the IFR’s multi-layered procedural safeguards. Aliens may file supplemental evidence if invited by the supervisory officer, and the officer must independently evaluate the record before issuing a final decision. These safeguards ensure that errors can be identified and corrected without necessitating formal, trial-type hearings. The Departments note that many administrative penalty and adjudication schemes, including certain USCIS benefit adjudications, and numerous civil penalty processes across the federal government operate without in-person hearings, and courts have recognized such frameworks as consistent with due process requirements. For these reasons, the Departments conclude that an in-person hearing is not required and that the written, supervisory review process established by the IFR provides respondents with a fair and meaningful opportunity to present their claims. DHS has designed the appeal process to ensure that an alien’s appeal is fairly and fully considered on the merits alone. Indeed, the IFR makes clear that any appeal of a penalty decision must be reviewed by a separate supervisory immigration officer who was not involved in the initial civil monetary penalty decision. Additionally, the IFR instructs the supervisory officer to review the appeal record de novo, which ensures that officers decide each appeal solely on the law and facts presented without providing any weight in favor of the initial decision. There is a presumption of regularity that attaches to the actions of government agencies, see U.S. Postal Serv., 534 U.S. at 10, and commenters have offered no support for their claim that immigration officers will deny otherwise meritorious challenges to civil penalties simply because they are employed by the same agency that issues and enforces these penalties.
Moreover, the Supreme Court has long held that it does not violate due process to combine enforcement and adjudication functions within a single agency. See Withrow v. Larkin, 421 U.S. 35, 56–58 (1975) (holding that the fact that the initial charge in an administrative proceeding is brought by the same agency that later adjudicates the matter is not, in and of itself, violative of due process); see also Marcello v. Bonds, 349 U.S. 302
(1955) (rejecting the argument that the former INS’s comingled prosecutorial and adjudicative functions, under a prior version of the INA, deprived aliens of due process in deportation proceedings). Thus, the Departments disagree with commenters’ suggestion that the consolidation of enforcement and adjudication functions within the same agency violates due process or inherently increases the risk of biased decision-making. The caselaw cited by one commenter does not undermine the Departments’ conclusion that the IFR’s appeal procedures comport with due process. At most, the cited cases stand for the principle that an agency’s adjudicatory procedures could violate due process if the adjudicator has a direct financial or pecuniary interest in the outcome of the case, has prejudged the case, or has some other concrete conflict of interest. These cases, however, do not support the commenter’s broad argument that DHS immigration officers cannot be neutral and unbiased merely because they are part of DHS. Rather the caselaw is clear: there is a presumption of honesty and integrity in administrative officials exercising adjudicative functions, even where there is a combination of investigative and adjudicative functions, and some evidence of actual bias must be presented to overcome this presumption. See Marcello, 349 U.S. at 311; Withrow, 421 U.S. at 47; see also Baran v. Port of Beaumont Navigation District, 57 F.3d 436, 446 (5th Cir. 1995). The commenter has not offered any actual and non-speculative reason to think that DHS officers cannot be neutral but will instead be “tempted” to impermissibly affirm penalty decisions issued by other DHS officers.

8. Civil Penalty Amounts; 8 CFR 281.1(g) Comment: Some commenters argued that the fines imposed by the IFR implicate the Eighth Amendment’s Excessive Fines Clause. They contend that, under U.S. Supreme Court precedent, even if a penalty is deemed “civil,” it constitutes punishment for purposes of the Excessive Fines Clause if it serves retributive or deterrent purposes, rather than remedial ones. They broadly claimed that the IFR authorizes DHS to issue fines that serve no remedial purpose, and the IFR’s self-expressed intent—to deter future unlawful entries and encourage greater compliance with removal and voluntary departure orders—reflects a punitive purpose. Commenters broadly stated that these penalties are excessive under the Eighth Amendment because they can result in sums “disproportionate” to the underlying immigration violations.
One commenter noted that there is a wide range of circumstances surrounding an alien’s decision to come to the United States or remain in the country after a removal order or voluntary departure order and suggested that the Departments’ failure to consider these circumstances violates the Excessive Fines Clause. The commenter listed examples, including aliens fleeing persecution or seeking a better life for their children, aliens who were unable to depart due to illness, financial problems, aliens with pending challenges to a removal order, and aliens who were removed in absentia and were unaware of the order. One commenter suggested that DHS should cap fines at levels that reflect the nature of the violation and an alien’s ability to pay.
Response: As an initial matter, the Departments note that the IFR did not change the scope or amount of the civil monetary penalties that Congress has established. It only changed the procedures for issuing and adjudicating these civil monetary penalties and for allowing an alien to contest them. See 90 FR 27454. DHS has imposed the maximum statutory penalties under both the IFR’s streamlined penalty process and the prior penalty process in 8 CFR part 280. This indicates that commenters’ Eighth Amendment concerns are directed primarily at the penalty amounts authorized by Congress, not at the IFR’s procedures, and are therefore outside of the scope of this rule. Nevertheless, in so far as commenters are suggesting the IFR is unlawful because it does not require DHS immigration officers to assess proportionality or consider mitigating factors before imposing the maximum penalty amount, the Departments respond as follows: (1) these civil monetary penalties do not fall within the Eighth Amendments Excessive Fines Clause, and (2) even if treated as “fines,” they are not excessive under the factors in United States v. Bajakajian, 524 U.S. 321 (1998).
The Supreme Court has never held that civil monetary penalties for immigration violations are “fines” for purposes of the Excessive Fines Clause. The Court’s cases in this area have either involved sanctions ordered as penalties for criminal conduct, or civil forfeiture actions imposed after a criminal conviction, where the property was used in the commission of the crime. See Alexander v. United States, 509 U.S. 544, 558 (1993) (criminal forfeiture of property); Bajakajian, 524 U.S. at 328 (criminal forfeiture of currency); Austin v. United States, 509 U.S. 602, 619–22 (1993) (civil forfeiture of property used to commit drug crimes). By contrast, the penalties covered by this rule are not imposed in connection with criminal offenses. They are civil measures designed to promote compliance with the immigration laws and support DHS’s enforcement efforts. The Supreme Court has long recognized that Congress may impose civil monetary penalties for violations of the Nation’s immigration laws, and that such penalties are not criminal in nature. See, e.g., Oceanic Steam Nav. Co. v. Stranahan, 214 U.S. 320, 337 (1909). Moreover, the fact that these penalties are intended to serve a deterrence purpose does not render them subject to the Excessive Fines Clause because “deterrence may serve civil as well as criminal goals.” Hudson v. United States, 522 U.S. 93 105, (1997) (internal quotation marks omitted); see also Garner v. U.S. Dep’t of Labor, 221 F.3d 822, 827 (5th Cir. 2000) (“That a statute serves to deter future conduct does not automatically render it punitive.”). In addition, Congress clearly intended these penalties to serve a remedial purpose. As explained in Section II of this preamble, apprehending, detaining, and removing removable aliens, and conducting section 240 removal proceedings imposes significant costs on the Departments, and a large number of unlawful entries strain resources at the border. Congress recognized these harms and authorized civil penalties to both encourage compliance with the immigration laws and to help fund DHS’s border security and removal efforts. See INA 280(b)(3), 8 U.S.C. 1330(b)(3) (providing that the Immigration Enforcement Account is available for removal of criminal aliens and structures along certain portions of the United States border); see also H. Rep. No. 104–469 pt. 1 at 155 (1996).
Even if these penalties were considered fines under the Excessive Fines Clause, DHS’s decision to impose the maximum amount authorized by Congress without considering each alien’s individual reasons for unlawful entry or failure to depart after a removal or departure order is neither excessive nor unconstitutional. Courts of appeals generally apply the following factors from Bajakajian, 524 U.S. at 337–40, to determine whether a fine is “grossly disproportional” to the gravity of an offense: (1) the amount of the penalty authorized by Congress and the nature of the harm that results from a violation; (2) the class of persons to whom the statute principally applies; and (3) a comparison with potential criminal penalties. Taken together, these factors support the conclusion that the IFR need not be amended to limit DHS’s authority to impose the maximum statutory penalties or to require a proportionality analysis before issuing a civil penalty or otherwise consider mitigating factors.
With respect to the first factor, judgments about the appropriate penalty amount for a violation “belong in the first instance to the legislature.” Bajakajian, 524 U.S. at 322. The civil monetary penalties covered by this rule reflect Congress’s considered judgment that the conduct involved—an alien’s unlawful entry or failure to depart after a removal or departure order—is particularly serious and warrants substantial fines to promote compliance with immigration laws. See 90 FR 27454. Consistent with Congress’s judgment and current enforcement priorities, DHS has determined that imposing the maximum authorized penalties is an appropriate response to the large number of aliens who have unlawfully entered the United States or have violated removal and departure orders in recent years. See Section II of this preamble. Courts have held that an agency’s civil penalty decision is not excessive under the Eighth Amendment when it does not exceed the statutory maximum. See Newell Recycling Co. v. EPA, 231 F.3d 204, 210 (5th Cir. 2000) (“No matter how excessive (in lay terms) an administrative fine may appear, if the fine does not exceed the limits prescribed by the statute authorizing it, the fine does not violate the Eighth Amendment”); see also Pharaon v. Bd. of Governors of the Fed. Res. Sys, 135 F.3d 148, 155–57 (D.C. Cir. 1998).
The specific statutorily authorized penalty amounts are also proportionate to the underlying immigration law violations. For the unlawful entry penalty, the maximum penalty (adjusted for inflation) is $500 for each entry. See 8 CFR 280.53(b)(15). That amount is not disproportionate to unlawful entries, including repeated unlawful entries, in violation of the immigration laws in light of Congress’s concerns when enacting this penalty. See H. Rep. No. 104–469 at 155 (1996) (noting that unlawful entry “with no consequences undermines the credibility of our efforts to secure the border”). For voluntary departure civil penalties, Congress has authorized Immigration Judges to set penalties between $1,992 and $9,970 (as adjusted for inflation). See 8 CFR 280.53(b)(3). Under longstanding regulations, Immigration Judges or the BIA must select an amount within that range that reflects the likelihood that the alien will comply with voluntary departure or may apply a presumptive amount of $3,000. See 8 CFR 1240.26(l); 73 FR 76927, 76936 (Dec. 18, 2008). DHS adheres to the amount set by EOIR. That amount is not excessive because it reflects both the adjudicator’s assessment of the incentive needed to secure compliance and Congress’s desire to ensure consequences for violating departure orders. See H. Rep. No. 104–469, at 122 (noting how aliens with voluntary departure and had “very little incentive” to actually depart the United States prior to IIRIRA).
For failure to depart after a removal order, Congress has authorized DHS to impose a penalty of $998 per day (as adjusted for inflation) for each day an alien willfully fails to depart. Commenters argue that these penalties are excessive because the cumulative total may, in some cases, exceed $1,000,000. The Departments disagree. Congress designed this provision so that each day of willful failure to depart constitutes a separate, repeating violation. See Section III.E. of this preamble. In this context, proportionality is assessed by looking at each per day penalty, not the cumulative amount. See e.g. United States v. Schwarzbaum, 127 F.4th 259, 276 (11th Cir. 2025). A $998 per diem penalty is not excessive compared to other immigration-related civil penalties. It reflects Congress’s judgment as to the seriousness of the harm that flows from each day an alien remains in the United States in willful violation of a removal order and how these willful violations undermine the credibility of the immigration laws and warrant a separate penalty.
Even when viewed cumulatively, the per diem failure to depart penalty is not excessive. Congress added this authority in IIRIRA to strengthen immigration enforcement and to increase the consequences for ignoring removal orders, in response to the problem of aliens remaining in the United States—sometimes for years—after final deportation orders. See H.R. Rep. No. 104–469, pt. 1, at 160 (1996) (explaining when enacting IIRIRA that “aliens who have final orders of deportation [often] remain in the United States indefinitely” and noting that “[a]s a result, the resources expended to identify, apprehend, and provide a hearing to a deportable alien are all too often wasted”). Against this backdrop, Congress expressly authorized DHS to impose per-day penalties that may reach substantial sums when an alien willfully remains in the United States for extended periods in violation of a removal order. In these circumstances, imposing the maximum daily amount, even where it results in a large total, is not excessive because the total directly reflects a long‑running, repeated violation. See Schwarzbaum, 127 F.4th at 284 (“when a cumulative fine is made up of constitutionally proportionate penalties, the cumulative fine “is, literally, directly proportionate to the offense”) (citation and internal quotations omitted).
Taken together, the three penalties covered by this rule reflect Congress’s calibrated assessment of the harm associated with each type of violation. Lower penalties for unlawful entry generally apply to aliens apprehended while entering and who are often subject to prompt return or expedited removal, thereby conserving resources associated with section 240 removal proceedings. Higher penalties for failure to depart reflect Congress’s judgment that remaining in the United States after a departure or removal order both seriously undermines the integrity of the immigration system and results in wasted enforcement and adjudicative resources. See H. Rep. No. 104 469, pt. 1, at 160. Accordingly, the Departments believe that imposing the maximum statutorily authorized amount of these penalties is both consistent with Congress’s purpose in enacting these penalties and not excessive given those amounts reflect the value of the harm Congress placed on such violations. See United States v. Chaplin’s, Inc., 646 F.3d 846, 852 (11th Cir. 2011) (“Congress, as a representative body, can distill the monetary value society places on harmful conduct”).
The second Bajakajian factor—whether the penalties apply to the class of persons for whom the statute was principally designed—also supports the Departments’ position. Aliens subject to these penalties fall squarely within the class Congress targeted. For unlawful entry penalties, an alien’s apprehension while attempting to enter the United States unlawfully is sufficient to support the penalty. For failure to depart penalties, ICE guidance instructs officers to confirm that the alien is subject to a final removal or voluntary departure order, that there is no DHS record of the alien’s departure, and that, based on available records, there are no legal impediments or other circumstances that would negate a willful or voluntary failure to depart. For per diem penalties, ICE guidance further requires officers to exclude periods when the alien was lawfully permitted to remain in the United States, such as during the pendency of a stay of removal. This is all Congress has required. Congress has clearly provided that any alien who engages in the proscribed conduct—unlawfully entering the United States or willfully or voluntarily failing to depart after a removal or departure order—is subject to a civil penalty up to the statutory maximum. By contrast to other immigration-related civil penalties53 and contrary to commenters’ suggestion, Congress has not required DHS to adjust the penalty based on proportionality or to consider specific mitigating circumstances. To the extent that commenters are suggesting that DHS must consider factors such as an alien’s inability to obtain travel documents, hospitalization, or other 53 See e.g., INA 274A(e)(5), 8 U.S.C. 1324a(e)(5) (listing various factors that must be considered when determining the appropriate amount of the penalty for certain employment-related immigration violations); INA 273(e), 8 U.S.C. 1323(e) (directing DHS to prescribe regulations for reducing carrier penalties for transporting undocumented aliens in appropriate circumstances).

However, the alien is best positioned to raise these considerations on appeal because DHS would generally not have knowledge of such circumstances. The second Bajakajian factor therefore supports DHS’s decision to assess these penalties up to the maximum statutorily authorized amount without first conducting a proportionality analysis or considering mitigating circumstances. The third Bajakajian factor—comparison to potential criminal sanctions— likewise weighs against a finding of excessiveness. In IIRIRA, Congress not only created the civil failure to depart penalty but also amended INA 243, 8 U.S.C. 1253, to make it a criminal offense for an alien to willfully fail or refuse to depart within 90 days of a final removal order. Congress authorized imprisonment of up to four years, a fine, or both, with enhanced penalties for certain criminal aliens. See INA 243(a)(1), 8 U.S.C. 1253(a)(1). 1. Statute of limitations Comment: Several commenters asserted that the rule does not explain how the federal statute of limitations applies to the civil penalties imposed under the IFR. One commenter suggested that the IFR’s streamlined penalty procedures make it difficult for aliens to raise statute of limitations defenses, and the complexity of these defenses undermines the IFR’s assumption about the readily verifiable and straightforward nature of these penalties. One commenter argued that the IFR does not require DHS to include information in the penalty decision about when the violation occurred which makes it difficult for aliens and their counsel to determine if DHS is correctly applying the statute of limitations. The commenter suggested that the 15-day appeal period, combined with the use of ordinary mail to serve decisions, could prevent aliens and their attorneys from investigating statute of limitations issues and raising the statute of limitations as a defense to these penalties. The commenter argued that eliminating BIA review is particularly problematic because DHS immigration officers do not have the legal expertise to resolve complex statute of limitations issues, and DHS does not have a mechanism to issue binding decisions on legal issues including those involving statutes of limitation. Commenters offered their views regarding how the federal statute of limitations, 28 U.S.C. 2462, applies to the penalties covered by this rule. For the failure to depart penalties, commenters claimed that the five-year statute of limitations begins to run the moment the voluntary departure period ends or when a removal order becomes final. They stated that DHS cannot impose fines for conduct that happened more than five years ago, such as remaining in the United States after a voluntary departure order period expired or after a removal order became final, particularly when there is no caselaw or statute recognizing a “continuing violation” theory for these penalties. For the unlawful entry penalties, commenters said that the limitations period begins to run once an alien is apprehended while in the process of entering or attempting to enter the United States. Response: The Departments disagree that the rule should be rescinded because it fails to include a statute of limitations provision. As an initial matter, the Departments emphasize the IFR’s limited scope. The IFR changed only the procedures by which DHS issues and adjudicates certain civil monetary penalties and the manner in which aliens may contest them. The IFR did not change or alter the criteria for issuing these penalties, including how DHS interprets or applies the general federal statute of limitations for these penalties. Indeed, the Departments note that none of the Departments’ other civil monetary penalty regulations contain a statute of limitations provision, including 8 CFR 280, which, prior to the IFR, governed the process for assessing the penalties covered by this rule. Accordingly, the Departments believe that it was reasonable and appropriate to limit the scope of the IFR consistent with the scope of the Departments’ other civil penalty regulations, especially because of the IFR’s narrow purpose of streamlining the process for the failure to depart and unlawful entry penalties. Therefore, the Departments decline to amend the rule to add a statute of limitations provision. For the same reasons, the Departments do not agree with commenters’ suggestion that failure to include a statute of limitations provisions renders the rule arbitrary or capricious.54 The Departments also disagree that the IFR’s streamlined proceedings make it too difficult for aliens to assert a statute of limitations defense. Consistent with 8 CFR 281.1(c)(1), each penalty decision lists the date when the alien became subject to a final removal or a voluntary departure order, or when the alien was apprehended entering or attempting to enter the United States unlawfully. See DHS Form 281, Notice of Violation and Order. Procedural Rule Exception Comment: Several commenters generally asserted that Departments lacked authority to issue the IFR without first publishing a notice of proposed rulemaking and providing a pre-promulgation comment period in accordance with the APA, 5 U.S.C. 553. First, commenters argued that the Departments cannot properly characterize the IFR as a procedural rule under 5 U.S.C. 553(b)(A). Commenters identified changes such as eliminating the NIF before issuing a final penalty, reducing the time to respond from 30 days to 15 days, allowing service by regular mail instead of certified or personal service, and moving appeals from the BIA to DHS. They asserted that these changes remove or weaken existing protections, implicate due process, change rights and obligations of affected individuals, and therefore render the rule substantive, which would require compliance with the APA’s notice and comment requirements.
Response: The Departments disagree. This rule was properly issued without prior notice and comment under 5 U.S.C. 553(b)(A), which exempts from those requirements “rules of agency organization, procedure, or practice.” Under the APA, notice and comment is required for substantive rules. By contrast, agency rules that prescribe agency organization, procedure, or practice are exempt from the notice-and-comment requirement. See 5 U.S.C. 553(b)(A). The “critical feature of the procedural rule exception is that it covers agency actions that do not themselves alter the rights or interests of parties, although it may alter the manner in which the parties present themselves or their viewpoints to the agency.” JEM Broadcasting Co., Inc. v. FCC, 22 F.3d 320, 328 (D.C. Cir. 1994) (cleaned up). In JEM Broadcasting, the D.C. Circuit upheld a rule that changed the timing and processing of applications, not the substantive criteria for deciding them, and treated the rule as procedural and exempt from notice and comment. Id.
As explained in the IFR, this rule fits within that procedural rule exemption. See 90 27454. This rule merely prescribes how the Departments conduct internal penalty proceedings and administrative reviews. The IFR does not alter the statutory obligations under INA 240B(d), 274D(a)(1), and 275(b); 8 U.S.C. 1229c(d), 1324d(a), and 1325(b), that authorize civil monetary penalties against aliens who fail to depart voluntarily, who willfully refuse to depart after a final order of removal, or who are apprehended while unlawfully entering or attempting to enter. It does not change the amount of these penalties or create new categories of violators or new statutory rights. The substantive legal liability remains as defined by statute.
Rather, the IFR merely revised the process by which the Departments impose these civil monetary penalties. For example, allowing DHS to serve penalty notices by regular mail (and no longer only by certified mail or personal service); shortening the period within which a respondent must contest the fine or exercise appeal rights from 30 to 15 days, moving the appeals process from the BIA to DHS, and other procedural streamlining merely alters the manner in which parties present themselves or their viewpoints to the Departments. Under the IFR, aliens continue to receive written notice of the penalty by regular mail and a meaningful opportunity to present a written response with supporting evidence before any final determination is made. Likewise, replacing BIA review with review by a DHS supervisory official who was not involved in the initial decision preserves neutral, independent oversight while streamlining the adjudication process. Accordingly, this rule is properly classified as a procedural rule and exempt from prior notice and comment procedures under the APA.
Moreover, though not required to do so, the Departments nonetheless solicited post-promulgation comments following publication of the IFR on June 27, 2025, and accepted comments through July 28, 2025.
