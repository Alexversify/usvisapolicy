DHS Cuts Refugee Status Termination Notice Response Time From 30 to 10 Days

Published Oct 13, 2026Effective Oct 14, 2026

DHS has issued an interim final rule amending 8 CFR Part 207 to shorten the response window for a Notice of Intent to Terminate (NOIT) refugee status from 30 days to 10 days. The rule becomes effective upon publication in the Federal Register, while also remaining open for public comment.

Key points

  • The response period for a Notice of Intent to Terminate (NOIT) refugee status is reduced from 30 days to 10 days.
  • This is an interim final rule, effective on the date it is published in the Federal Register, not yet a final, settled rule.
  • Public comments are due 30 days after the publication date; the exact calendar date is not specified in the source text.
  • Comments must be submitted in English through regulations.gov, referencing DHS Docket No. USCIS-2026-0397.
  • DHS cites concerns about weakened vetting during a prior administration and related fraud investigations as justification for the change.
  • The underlying eligibility criteria for refugee status are not changed, only the timeline for responding to a termination notice.

Who is affected

People already admitted to the United States as refugees who may receive a termination notice, and their attorneys.

What to do

Affected individuals and their attorneys should be prepared to respond to any Notice of Intent to Terminate within the new 10 day window, and interested parties may submit comments via regulations.gov within 30 days of publication.

II. Executive Summary A. Purpose of the Regulatory Action The purpose of this rulemaking is to shorten the termination notice period in order to mitigate potential risks posed by aliens admitted to the United States as refugees when, in fact, they were not eligible for refugee classification at the time of admission. Those who are improperly admitted may not have their identity fully confirmed and vetted due to an incomplete record, and as such pose a risk to program integrity and may pose a risk to national security and public safety. A review of refugee processing policies and procedures implemented under the Biden administration indicates an alarming erosion of integrity leading to aliens being admitted to the United States as refugees when their identities, eligibility for refugee classification, and admissibility were determined based on incomplete records.1 Operational guidance implemented during this period allowed officers to forgo typical lines of inquiry, sacrificing the caliber and efficacy of the refugee interview, a critical screening component of the refugee adjudication process.2 The priority of expediency over quality encouraged a lack of robust interviews. III. Background and Purpose A. Initial Refugee Processing Refugee status in the United States is a highly sought after immigration benefit for displaced persons throughout the world due in part to the benefits it bestows as well as a pathway to permanent status. Resettlement to the United States through the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) is a multi-step process involving several agency partners. The U.S. Department of State (State) leads the annual consultations process, funds and oversees refugee resettlement activities, contracts with the non- governmental organizations that provide pre- and post-USCIS interview support, and facilitates the Priority 1 referrals to the USRAP process. After an alien is determined to have access to the USRAP, they may file a Form I-590, Registration for Classification as Refugee.10 Applications for refugee classification are primarily processed overseas and adjudicated by USCIS Refugee Officers who determine whether an alien meets the refugee definition and is otherwise eligible for resettlement to the United States. Under United States law, to be eligible for refugee classification an applicant must establish that he or she:
•is located outside the country of his or her nationality; 11 •is of special humanitarian concern to the United States;
•was persecuted or fears persecution due to race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group;
•is not firmly resettled in another country; and •is admissible as an immigrant to the United States.

B. Termination of Refugee Status The sole basis for USCIS to terminate the status of an alien conditionally admitted to the United States as a refugee is if USCIS determines that the alien did not meet the definition of a refugee at the time of admission to the United States.15 No other ground of termination is specified under the law. An alien who did not, in fact, meet the refugee definition at the time of admission would have been inadmissible, and once that status is terminated, will generally lack legal grounds to remain in the United States.16 The determination to terminate refugee status because the alien did not meet the definition of a refugee at the time of his or her admission to the United States applies solely to principal refugees and not to derivative refugees. In 2017, President Trump issued Executive Order 13769, Protecting the Nation from Foreign Terrorist Entry into the United States, restricting entry to the U.S. by certain foreign nationals and temporarily suspending the USRAP.35 During this suspension of refugee processing agencies were directed to review and strengthen vetting procedures. Gathering testimony on both past persecution and well- founded fear ensures a thorough and accurate evaluation of the applicant’s eligibility and prevents overlooking critical facts that could substantiate, or call into question, their claim. For example, thorough elicitation of testimony regarding past and future harm may help identify inconsistencies and potential fraud by allowing adjudicators to probe the alien’s narrative for credibility by ensuring it is internally consistent and consistent with statements the alien may have provided to processing partners prior to interview. Most importantly, it requires officers to probe more thoroughly into an applicant’s background and experiences, helping to establish his or her identity, and further ensure that the correct decision is made in his or her case. In addition to the relaxed requirements for principal refugee applicants, USCIS changed policies regarding derivative applicants. The first Trump administration issued guidance emphasizing the importance of eliciting testimony from derivative I-590 applicants apart from the principal refugee applicant to further explore potential national security, identity, inadmissibility, and credibility issues. However, USCIS subsequently rescinded this guidance under the Biden administration and provided refugee officers the sole discretion on when to interview derivative applicants separately. The purpose of this change was to reduce the amount of questions and time spent with derivatives in an interview, thus not thoroughly vetting and identifying potential issues of all members of a case who, upon case approval, were eligible to travel to the United States as refugees along with the principal applicant.
Compounding the issue, marriage guidance under the previous administration relaxed the criteria for adding a derivative spouse to a case. Although intended to accommodate couples who genuinely acted as spouses but could not formalize their union due to local restrictions, the standards were misapplied, allowing individuals with minimal or short-term connections to be classified as spouses and included on cases. This practice, combined with lowered interview standards and increasingly brief, less detailed case reviews in the name of streamlining, increased the risk of approving applicants with only tenuous relationships with the principal applicant.
In parallel with this minimized focus on eliciting substantive information to comprehensively understand an alien’s background and identify potential issues of concern, the increased interview quotas made it more difficult for officers to explore important issues, fully probe the alien’s credibility, and still meet their productivity expectations. During the first Trump administration, USCIS reduced the number of refugee interviews conducted per day per officer for certain cases to allow for further exploration of potential national security, inadmissibility, and credibility issues at interviews for refugee adjudications. Notwithstanding, USCIS under the Biden administration, in their effort to streamline interviews and increase the volume of refugee cases that could be processed, increased the number of cases an officer was expected to interview and adjudicate in a day for certain cases, again pressuring officers to at times scale back interviews to comply with the expectations.
This shift away from in-depth, thorough interviews in order to streamline and expedite admission of aliens to the United States as refugees shut down important lines of questioning to identify potential national security, public safety, and other concerns to accurately assess eligibility, illustrating the program’s emphasis on the volume of refugee case completions rather than the quality of interviews or the integrity of adjudications. Refugee officers potentially missed vital information that could impact an applicant’s eligibility, including issues related to fraud, public safety, and national security, and discretionary factors. USCIS, under the Biden administration, failed to fulfill its responsibility to ensure that its officers thoroughly assessed applicants, potentially resulting in an influx of ineligible aliens into the country that may pose a risk to the national interests of the United States.
Moreover, USCIS under the Biden administration decreased requirements for supervisory review of refugee cases by only requiring review of the assessment in lieu of reviewing the entire case contents. This limited supervisor oversight to ensure cases were thoroughly and accurately adjudicated, increased the risk that important information about an alien was overlooked.

IV. Discussion of Interim Final Rule Previous administrations’ prioritization of the admissions of large numbers of refugees over homeland security blatantly overlooked essential components of eligibility and put at risk the national security, public safety and other interests of the U.S. citizenry. Safeguarding national security and public safety remains the foremost priority of the country’s immigration programs, taking precedence over numerical admission targets. The agency stands committed to conducting rigorous vetting of all aliens and to the detection and prevention of fraud and other serious threats, ensuring the safety of all Americans prior to admission of an alien to the United States.
Accordingly, this IFR shortens the amount of time that refugees are provided to respond to a NOIT in order to ensure that terminations of refugee status can occur as expeditiously as possible to protect national security and public safety, given the risks created by the deficient vetting described above in Section III.C. Under this IFR, refugees will continue to receive a NOIT, which will specify DHS’s intent to terminate their refugee status based on a determination that they did not meet the refugee definition at the time of admission. DHS will provide the specific reason or reasons why USCIS found the refugee failed to meet the refugee definition at the time of admission (e.g., failure to establish that past harm experienced was serious enough to rise to the level of persecution, a determination that the alien’s harm was not on account of a protected ground, failure to provide credible testimony as to any or all of the elements of the refugee definition, etc.). However, this rule will provide such aliens with ten days (rather than 30 days) to respond (provide documentary evidence or indicate a desire to provide oral evidence) if they choose. If the deadline to reply to the NOIT passes without a response, the refugee is considered to have forfeited his or her right to rebut the allegations in the NOIT, and DHS will terminate his or her refugee status.
By regulation, the response period begins either upon personal service of the NOIT directly to the alien or, if sent by mail, upon mailing of the NOIT.52 When calculating the response deadline, all days, including Saturdays, Sundays, and federal holidays, are counted.53 However, if the final day falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or federal holiday, the deadline is extended to the next business day.54 Per 8 CFR 103.8(b), if the notice is served by mail, three days are added to the response period, with service considered complete on the date the notice is mailed.
DHS’s foremost priority is the safety and security of American citizens and residents. At the same time, DHS is committed to ensuring that refugees who receive a NOIT are afforded a meaningful opportunity to respond or provide a rebuttal. To enhance mail service reliability and afford refugees the full response period, DHS will utilize express delivery service providers, which guarantee a three-day delivery window and offer tracking and confirmation of both the NOIT’s departure from DHS custody and its delivery to the recipient.55 This approach ensures that refugees receive their NOIT promptly and can exercise their right to respond. Reducing the 30-day response timeframe for NOITs issued in refugee terminations to a 10-day response timeframe allows DHS to take swifter action in cases where aliens did not meet the definition of a refugee when admitted to the United States. USCIS needs the ability to terminate refugee status in such cases in order to more quickly begin the removal process for aliens who should never have been admitted to the United States as refugees in the first place, including aliens similarly situated to those discussed above in Section III.C, who also pose a national security or public safety risk. While termination of refugee status is not a precondition for initiation of removal proceedings, the termination of refugee status gives DHS a statutory basis for removal, and in some cases, no other removal grounds apply, so this termination is needed.56 Accordingly, promptly terminating refugee status in cases where the alien was clearly ineligible at the time of admission facilitates DHS’s ability to process the alien for removal under sections 235, 240, and 241 of the INA, and to remove the alien if they do not qualify for relief from removal.

More broadly, timely termination of their refugee status is critical to the integrity of the country’s immigration programs, considering the risks they may pose to the United States. As USCIS continues to encounter aliens admitted as refugees who did not meet the definition of refugee at the time of admission, termination of refugee status in as prompt and efficient manner as possible ensures USCIS can best protect the safety and security of Americans and continue complying with E.O.s 14161 and 14163. By shortening the 30-day period for an alien to respond to a NOIT to 10 days, USCIS is exercising its authority to expeditiously terminate the status of refugees who did not meet the refugee definition at the time of their admission to the United States. Streamlining the termination process additionally ensures that ineligible aliens who were erroneously allowed to enter the United States as refugees lose their authorization to work in as timely a manner as possible since termination of refugee status also results in termination of employment authorization.
DHS believes that reducing the NOIT response period from 30 days to 10 days maintains adequate procedural safeguards for aliens by affording them an opportunity to respond to the NOIT while also balancing the government’s interests in national security and public safety and in protecting the integrity of the refugee program. The NOIT will inform the alien the basis for termination and explain that they may submit evidence to contest termination, and/or request an interview. An alien does not need to submit evidence or a full response within the 10 days, and may instead opt for an interview, but must make the interview request to USCIS within the response period. Aliens who receive a NOIT under the proposed process therefore will continue to receive a meaningful opportunity to respond to the allegations in the notice—the alien can submit a written response to the NOIT within the deadline and/or request an interview with USCIS where their refugee claim can be heard. The abbreviated time period to respond to the NOIT will in turn allow the government to more efficiently process cases where aliens did not meet the refugee definition at the time of admission to the United States, and who were from the outset, never eligible for the benefit they received. Instead of waiting 30 days for an alien to respond to a NOIT before scheduling an interview if requested by the alien or taking other action, this rule will allow USCIS to act as soon as 10 days after service of the NOIT. For aliens who do not respond timely, the 10-day response period will allow USCIS to finalize termination much more quickly. Furthermore, each alien whose refugee status is terminated will be processed under INA sections 235, 240, or 241 and may seek other forms of relief or protection from removal as permitted in those proceedings. For example, aliens in section 240 removal proceedings may seek other forms of relief in a hearing before an immigration judge where the interests of the U.S. Government are properly represented by an attorney from the U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) Office of the Principal Legal Advisor (OPLA).

Likewise, section 553(d)'s requirement of 30-day advance publication may be waived by the agency for good cause found and published with the rule. See 5 U.S.C. 553(d)(3).
The “impracticable” prong of the good cause exception excuses notice and comment in emergency situations, or where the delay caused by the APA's notice-and- comment procedures would result in serious harm to life, property, or an immediate threat to public safety.57 Although the good cause exception is “narrowly construed and only reluctantly countenanced,”58 it is an important safety valve to be used where delay caused by notice and comment would do real harm (even absent an emergency situation).59 An agency may find that advance notice and comment or a delayed effective date is “impracticable” when undertaking such procedure would impede due and timely execution of an important agency function.60 For example, courts have explained that notice-and-comment rulemaking may be impracticable where, for instance, air travel security would be unable to address threats posing a “possible imminent hazard to aircraft, persons and property within the United States;”61 if a rule was of life-saving importance to mine workers in the event of a mine explosion;62 if public safety is 57 See Nat. Engaging in the APA’s notice-and-comment requirement under 5 U.S.C. 553(b) would impede DHS’s duties as outlined in E.O. 14161, requiring that sufficient safeguards are in place to prevent any refugee from being admitted to the United States without undergoing stringent vetting, and E.O. 14163, permitting admission of refugees on a case-by-case basis only so long as it is determined to be in the national interest and the alien does not pose a threat to the security and welfare of the United States. Thus, in accordance with President Trump's policy determinations related to foreign nationals, DHS is taking, without delay, immediate action to ensure that aliens who did not meet the refugee definition at the time of admission to the United States are vetted and screened to the maximum degree possible to ensure they do not receive significant benefits, including employment authorization, permanent legal status, and other federal, state, and local benefits. Furthermore, unless DHS acts immediately, there is a risk that aliens who were not eligible for admission as refugees could present harm to the public interest.
As explained in detail in Section IV of this preamble, DHS reasonably believes, based on a review of prior refugee approvals and internal procedures, policies, and directives, that the lack of detailed refugee interviews and insufficient inquiry into critical matters such as inadmissibilities or terrorism-related grounds, coupled with the prioritization of admission numbers over legally sufficient decisions and other programmatic issues like over-reliance on COI, have resulted in aliens receiving refugee status when they were not eligible. These practices have compromised the integrity of the refugee program.
In Operation PARRIS, DHS has further identified similar issues regarding the quality of refugee interviews after reviewing a subset of cases, where in some instances populations from specific countries were processed and approved with minimal lines of questioning. This rule amends the 30-day period to 10 days for aliens to respond to a NOIT so that the agency can expeditiously issue terminations in cases where, after a thorough review of the case and comprehensive vetting, USCIS determines that the alien did not meet the refugee definition at the time of admission. In such cases, an alien will not maintain valid refugee status a day longer than legally required. This IFR would accordingly more quickly eliminate a pathway to permanent status used by aliens with falsified claims or malevolent intent, including those who may pose an ongoing and imminent threat to public safety and national security such as the refugee cases reviewed and terminated by the USCIS Vetting Center outlined above.
Therefore, if DHS had to engage in advance notice-and-comment procedures, it would continue to allow aliens who should have never been admitted to the United States as refugees to remain present longer than necessary, potentially putting at risk the safety and security of the United States and compromising the integrity of the U.S. immigration system. For example, in one case, after admission to the U.S. it was later discovered that the refugee was a war criminal who should have been subject to the persecutor bar and not allowed into the country. In another case listed above, the refugee was not admissible due to a lack of credibility with regard to both participation in and sending money to the OLF (a Tier III terrorist organization at the time of the alien’s involvement) as well as his father’s status as a well-known OLF member. In these circumstances, providing advance notice-and-comment procedures is impracticable and not in the public interest. Finally, DHS finds that it is unnecessary to delay this final rule’s effective date under 5 U.S.C. 553(d). There is no need to give affected parties additional time to adjust their behavior before this final rule takes effect because DHS believes there are likely no behavioral adjustments to make. The aliens who would be affected by this rule have already entered the United States.69 In other words, a delay in the rule’s effective date would be unlikely to have an impact on an alien’s choice to seek to enter the United States as a refugee or not and would not impact whether or not they were eligible for such status at the time of admission. A delayed effective date would serve no purpose but create further harm to the public, as exemplified by the cases that, upon review and additional vetting, have resulted in the issuance of NOITs because the record indicated the alien was not a refugee at the time of admission.
For the reasons outlined above, DHS believes that bypassing the ordinary notice- and-comment procedures and the delayed effective date requirement is justified, under the totality of circumstances, and given that immediate action is necessary, is consistent with DHS’s statutory mission to take regulatory action to administer the refugee program effectively, as well as the President’s directives in E.O. 14161 and E.O. 14163. Nevertheless, recognizing the value of public comments, DHS is publishing this rule as an IFR with a request for public comment.

§ 207.9 Termination of refugee status.
The refugee status of any alien (and of the spouse or child of the alien) admitted to the United States under section 207 of the Act will be terminated by USCIS if the alien was not a refugee within the meaning of section 101(a)(42) of the Act at the time of admission. USCIS will notify the alien in writing of its intent to terminate the alien's refugee status. The alien will have 10 days from the date notice is served upon him or her in accordance with 8 CFR 103.8 to present written or oral evidence to show why the alien’s refugee status should not be terminated. There is no appeal under this chapter from the termination of refugee status by USCIS. Upon termination of refugee status, USCIS will process the alien under sections 235, 240, and 241 of the Act.
